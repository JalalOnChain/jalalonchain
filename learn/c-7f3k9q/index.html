Lesson 01 · about 10 minutes

Money, and why crypto exists

Money is a shared record of who owns what. Crypto is a different way of keeping that record: one that no single bank or company controls.

Money is mostly a record

Most of your money isn't notes in a drawer. It's a number in your bank's database. When you pay someone by UPI or card, no cash moves. Two banks update their records: your balance goes down and theirs goes up. You trust the banks to keep those records honest, keep them running all day, and settle up with each other.

That system works well most of the time, but it has limits. Payments stop at borders and bank holidays. A middleman can freeze, delay or refuse a payment. And everyone has to trust whoever holds the record.

Think of it like this

The village notebook

Picture a village with no bank. Every payment is written in a notebook: "Asha pays Ben 5." Instead of one person keeping the notebook, every household keeps an identical copy. If Ben says Asha never paid, anyone can check. If someone scribbles a fake entry in their copy, it stops matching everyone else's, and the village ignores it. That shared notebook is the idea behind Bitcoin.

Where Bitcoin came from

In October 2008, during the global financial crisis, someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a nine-page paper describing "a peer-to-peer electronic cash system". The network went live in January 2009. Instead of a bank keeping the record, thousands of computers around the world keep copies of the same ledger and agree on every new entry.

Since then, thousands of other cryptocurrencies have launched. Some, like Ethereum, added programs that run on the ledger. Others, like USDT, are digital dollars that ride on top of existing chains.

What crypto is good at

  • Sending value anywhere, any hour, without asking a bank
  • A record anyone can check for themselves
  • Owning an asset directly, without a middleman holding it for you

What it isn't

  • Not guaranteed. Prices can fall fast and far
  • Not reversible. A mistaken payment usually can't be undone
  • Not invisible. Most blockchains are public for anyone to read
Look closer

People often call crypto "anonymous". A better word is pseudonymous. Names aren't recorded, but every payment is public and permanent, tied to an address. You'll see exactly what that looks like in lesson 6.

Lesson 02 · about 15 minutes

How a blockchain works

A blockchain is the village notebook, run by computers. New payments are grouped into pages called blocks, and each page is sealed with a fingerprint of the page before it.

Transactions

A transaction is a signed message: "move this amount from my address to that one." It's broadcast to the network and waits in a queue called the mempool.

Blocks

Every so often a batch of waiting transactions is packed into a block and added to the end of the chain. Bitcoin adds a block about every 10 minutes, Ethereum every 12 seconds.

Hashes

A hash is a short fingerprint of any data. Change a single letter and the fingerprint changes completely. Each block stores the hash of the block before it, which chains them together.

Who adds blocks

On Bitcoin, miners compete with computing power (proof of work). On Ethereum, validators lock up ETH as a deposit (proof of stake). Either way, they earn fees and rewards for doing it honestly.

Try to rewrite history

Each block below holds some payments and the fingerprint of the block before it. Edit any payment. Watch which blocks break, and notice the damage spreads to every block after it. To get away with a change, you'd have to redo every later block faster than the rest of the network, which is what makes old entries so hard to alter.

All four blocks link up.

Confirmations

Once your transaction is in a block, it has one confirmation. Each new block added on top adds another. The deeper it's buried, the safer it is. That's why exchanges wait for a few confirmations before crediting a deposit.

Lesson 03 · about 15 minutes

Wallets, keys and seed phrases

A crypto wallet doesn't hold coins. The coins stay on the blockchain. A wallet holds the keys that let you move them.

Think of it like this

A glass letterbox

Your address is a glass letterbox on a public street. Anyone can see it, anyone can drop money in, and anyone can see how much is inside. Your private key is the only key that opens it. Lose the key and the money is stuck there forever. Give someone a copy and they can empty it.

Address

Public, safe to share. It's what you give someone so they can pay you. Bitcoin addresses often start with bc1, Ethereum's with 0x.

Private key

Secret. It signs your transactions. Whoever has it controls the funds, no questions asked.

Seed phrase

12 or 24 ordinary words that can regenerate all your keys. It's a master backup: write it on paper, keep it offline, never type it into a website.

Wallet app

Software or a device that stores your keys and signs for you. Examples: MetaMask, Trust Wallet, a Ledger hardware wallet.

Two ways to hold crypto

On an exchange (custodial)In your own wallet (self-custody)
Who holds the keysThe exchangeYou
Forgot your password?Reset it with supportRestore from your seed phrase, or it's gone
Main riskThe exchange fails, freezes withdrawals or gets hackedYou lose the seed phrase or get tricked into sharing it
Good forBuying, selling, small amounts while you learnHolding long term, using apps on the blockchain

You'll also hear hot wallets (keys on an internet-connected device, convenient) and cold wallets (keys kept offline, safer for savings).

Stay safe

No real exchange, wallet company or support agent will ever ask for your seed phrase. Anyone who asks for it is trying to steal from you. The same goes for "wallet sync" or "validation" websites that ask you to type it in.

Lesson 04 · about 12 minutes

Coins, tokens and stablecoins

Not every crypto asset is the same kind of thing. Some are built into a blockchain, some are created by programs running on top of one, and some are designed to always be worth one dollar.

Coins

The built-in currency of a blockchain, used to pay its fees. BTC on Bitcoin, ETH on Ethereum, TRX on Tron, SOL on Solana.

Tokens

Assets created by a program (a smart contract) on an existing chain. Anyone can create one in minutes, which is why there are millions. Examples: USDT, USDC, UNI, LINK.

Stablecoins

Tokens built to hold a steady price, usually $1. USDT and USDC are backed by cash and short-term government bonds held by the company that issues them.

NFTs

Tokens where each one is unique, like a numbered ticket. Used for digital art, collectibles and memberships.

Think of it like this

A country's currency vs. gift vouchers

A coin is like a country's own currency: it's built into the system and you need it to pay the fees. A token is like a gift voucher printed by a shop that operates inside that country. The voucher only means something because the shop's records say so.

Same name, different chains

USDT exists on Ethereum, Tron, Solana and many other chains. Each version lives in its own contract on its own chain. USDT on Tron is not the same thing as USDT on Ethereum, and you can't send one directly to the other network. Lesson 5 shows why that matters when you send.

Stay safe

Because anyone can create a token, anyone can create one called "USDT". Fake tokens show up in wallets looking exactly like the real thing. What makes a token real is its contract address, not its name. Lesson 8 shows you how to check it.

Lesson 05 · about 15 minutes

Buying, sending and fees

Most people's first crypto comes from an exchange. The trickiest part isn't buying. It's sending it somewhere else without making an expensive mistake.

A typical first journey

Sign up on an exchangeYou verify your identity (KYC), much like opening a bank account.
Add money and buyDeposit rupees or dollars and buy BTC, ETH or USDT. At this point the exchange holds it for you.
Withdraw to a walletPaste your wallet address, pick the network, confirm. This is a real on-chain transaction.
Wait for confirmationsYour wallet shows it once it's in a block. A few more blocks and it's settled.

Before you hit send

  • Pick the right network. Sending USDT on Tron to an address that expects Ethereum can lose the funds. Sender and receiver must use the same network.
  • Check the whole address. Compare it character by character, not just the first and last few.
  • Send a small test first. For large amounts, send a little, confirm it arrived, then send the rest.
  • There's no undo. Once confirmed, a transaction can't be reversed. Only the receiver can send it back.

Why fees exist

Block space is limited. Fees pay the miners or validators who include your transaction, and they rise when lots of people want in at once. On Bitcoin the fee depends on how many bytes your transaction takes up. On Ethereum it depends on how much computing it needs, measured in "gas". You always pay the fee in the chain's own coin, so you need a little ETH to send USDT on Ethereum, and a little TRX to send it on Tron.

Stay safe

Scammers send tiny or zero-value transfers from addresses that look almost like ones you've used, so a fake address appears in your history. If you copy from history without checking, you pay the scammer. Copy addresses from a source you trust and check every character.

Lesson 06 · about 20 minutes

Reading a Bitcoin transaction

Bitcoin doesn't keep balances. It keeps a pile of unspent coins of different sizes, and every transaction spends some of them and creates new ones. Once that clicks, every Bitcoin explorer page makes sense.

Think of it like this

Paying with notes, getting change

You owe a shop ₹300 and you have a ₹500 note. You can't tear the note, so you hand over all ₹500 and get ₹200 back. Bitcoin works the same way. The coins you hold are called UTXOs (unspent transaction outputs). A transaction spends whole UTXOs as inputs and creates new ones as outputs: one for the person you're paying and usually one for your change.

Example 1: the first payment between two people

On 12 January 2009, Satoshi Nakamoto sent 10 BTC to Hal Finney, a programmer testing the brand-new software. It sits in block 170.

On 22 May 2010, Laszlo Hanyecz paid 10,000 BTC for two pizzas, which is why 22 May is now Bitcoin Pizza Day. Look at the shape: he had mined lots of small coins, so his wallet had to gather 131 of them to reach 10,000.

This is what most transactions look like: one coin in, two out. One output pays someone, the other returns the change. Here the sender sent the change straight back to the same address, which makes it easy to tell which is which.

The one equation to remember

Inputs = outputs + fee. Bitcoin has no fee field. The fee is whatever is left over. Check it on example 3: 39,975,281 − 210,196 − 39,763,957 = 1,128 sats.

One bitcoin is 100,000,000 satoshis (sats). Explorers switch between the two, so get comfortable converting.

Look closer

When a transaction spends several inputs at once, one wallet had to sign for all of them, so they almost always belong to the same owner. And spotting which output is change tells you where the money actually went. Keep both in mind whenever you read a Bitcoin transaction.

Lesson 07 · about 15 minutes

Reading an Ethereum transaction

Ethereum swaps Bitcoin's coins-and-change for something closer to a bank account. Each address has a balance, and a transaction moves value from one to another. It also numbers every transaction an account sends and charges for computing in "gas".

Think of it like this

A bank account with numbered cheques

An Ethereum address works like a bank account: no notes, no change, just a balance going up and down. Every outgoing payment carries a cheque number, called the nonce, starting at 0. Cheque 5 can't clear before cheque 4. The fee is like paying a courier by distance: plain transfers are short trips, complicated contract calls cost more.

Units

1 ETH = 1,000,000,000 gwei = 1018 wei. Fees are quoted in gwei. Raw data from an API is in wei.

The fee formula

Fee = gas used × gas price. A plain ETH transfer always uses exactly 21,000 gas.

Example: the first ETH ever transferred

Ethereum launched on 30 July 2015, and transfers were switched on about a week later. Block 46147 holds the first one. The amount, 31,337 wei, is a programmers' in-joke ("elite" in hacker slang), and the fee was huge by today's standards.

Look closer

The nonce tells you a lot for free. A nonce of 0 means you're looking at an account's very first transaction. A nonce in the tens of thousands means a machine sent it, usually an exchange's wallet, not a person clicking send.

Lesson 08 · about 20 minutes

Token transfers

Most value moving on Ethereum today isn't ETH, it's tokens like USDT. This is where people misread explorers most often: the "To" field usually shows the token's contract, not the person who got paid.

Think of it like this

A vending machine with a ledger inside

A smart contract is a program that lives at its own address. The USDT contract keeps a private ledger of who owns how many dollars. To send USDT you don't send money to the other person. You send a message to the contract saying "move 129 of mine to this address," and the contract updates its ledger.

Example: a USDT transfer

Look closely at where the real recipient shows up. It isn't the To field.

Three places a token transfer shows up

  1. Input data. The raw instruction, transfer(to, amount), encoded as hex. Explorers decode it when the contract's code is verified.
  2. Event logs. The contract announces a Transfer(from, to, value) event. Explorers build their token-transfer lists from these.
  3. Token transfers section. The friendly summary at the top of the explorer page. Start here, then check the details below it.

Decimals

Contracts only store whole numbers. USDT uses 6 decimals, so a raw value of 129128964 means 129.128964 USDT. Most other tokens use 18. Always check a token's decimals before reading a raw number.

Stay safe

In the same block as this example, another transaction made ten USDT transfers of exactly 0 between unrelated addresses (0x6dcc18…27979f). That's the address-poisoning trick from lesson 5, happening for real. And to check a token is genuine, open its contract: real USDT on Ethereum is 0xdAC17F958D2ee523a2206206994597C13D831ec7.

Lesson 09 · about 20 minutes

Other chains

Bitcoin and Ethereum give you two models: coins with change, and accounts with balances. Almost every other chain is a variation on one of them. Spot which family a chain belongs to and you can read it in minutes.

Tron: where most USDT moves

Tron is an account-based chain with addresses starting with T. Its version of USDT (often written USDT-TRC20) is hugely popular for payments because transfers are fast and cheap. It works just like lesson 8: you call the USDT contract and it updates its ledger.

The difference is fees. Tron charges in two resources, energy (for running contracts) and bandwidth (for data size). You can cover them by staking TRX, renting energy, or simply burning a little TRX.

The Ethereum family: one address, many chains

BNB Smart Chain, Polygon, Arbitrum, Base, Optimism, Avalanche and many others copy Ethereum's design. Same 0x addresses, same transaction fields, same token logic, each with its own Etherscan-style explorer (bscscan.com, polygonscan.com, arbiscan.io, basescan.org).

The same private key controls the same 0x address on all of them. That's handy, but it's also why picking the right network when you send matters so much.

Solana: accounts that hold accounts

Solana addresses are long strings of letters and numbers with no fixed prefix. Its twist: your wallet doesn't hold tokens directly. It owns a separate token account for each token. Explorers like Solscan show both the token account that changed and the wallet that owns it.

Solana transactions are identified by a signature rather than a hash, and one transaction often bundles many steps. Start with the explorer's balance-change summary.

Open the USDC page on Solscan and click any recent transfer to see token accounts and owners side by side.
